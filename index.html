Abstract

Embodied agents must decide both where to look and when the evidence warrants a decision. SafeVantage is a semantic memory and active-perception policy that records the views supporting each claim. The policy uses this evidence state to select corroborating views and to output Yes, No, or Abstain.

On a held-out ProcTHOR test spanning 232 unseen houses and 7,424 paired episodes per method and action budget, SafeVantage raises macro-F1 from 0.4845 to 0.6044 and reduces travel from 7.23 to 4.94 m at eight actions, while risk falls from 0.3964 to 0.3842. At twelve actions, macro-F1 rises from 0.5855 to 0.6559, risk falls from 0.3685 to 0.3649, and travel falls from 8.42 to 7.44 m.

On a separate 96-house cohort, removing supporting-view identity, target-ray alignment, and yaw diversity lowers macro-F1 by 4.35 points at eight actions and 1.15 at twelve. Equal-input HM3D comparisons show lower selective risk, and ScanNet interventions show that answer quality decreases when supporting views are removed and recovers when they are restored.

A memory for deciding what an embodied agent has seen, what remains uncertain, and which view should come next.

Method

For each scene, SafeVantage captures posed observations containing RGB, optional depth, camera pose, and time. A semantic claim is represented by its supporting views, support count, and optional 3D target estimate. This record preserves which observations justify a claim and distinguishes one weak observation from a repeated spatial pattern.

The memory separates target visibility, property or relation sufficiency, search coverage, and answer correctness. Before a provisional detection, the policy balances spatial novelty, category-room evidence, and travel. After a detection, it seeks a corroborating view from a distinct position and uses the resulting evidence state for the final decision. The selective head emits Yes, No, or Abstain under calibrated thresholds.

Results

We evaluate SafeVantage on 232 unseen ProcTHOR houses, with 7,424 paired episodes per method and action budget. Five acquisition baselines share the same scenes, detector predictions, candidate viewpoints, and starts. Comparisons use equal action budgets, with the primary baseline selected on validation data. Confidence intervals resample whole houses to account for related episodes within each scene.

At eight actions, SafeVantage raises macro-F1 from 0.4845 to 0.6044 and answer rate from 37.72% to 58.07% relative to the category–room prior. Mean travel falls by 31.7%, from 7.23 to 4.94 m, and risk decreases from 0.3964 to 0.3842. At twelve actions, macro-F1 rises from 0.5855 to 0.6559 against detector-confidence gain, while answer rate increases from 51.63% to 63.89%. Risk falls from 0.3685 to 0.3649 and travel from 8.42 to 7.44 m.

SafeVantage achieves the highest macro-F1 and answer rate and the lowest risk among the evaluated policies at both budgets. It also travels less than four of the five baselines. Nearest frontier travels least, while SafeVantage achieves stronger decisions by directing movement toward informative observations.

The trajectory example shows how an initial partial detection becomes corroborated evidence. From the same start and with the same eight-action budget, SafeVantage gathers four supporting views, answers correctly, and travels 5.4 m. The category–room prior misses the target and travels 13.7 m. Retaining the observations behind a claim gives the next acquisition step a concrete target to investigate.

Ablations help explain this behavior. On 96 validation houses, jointly removing supporting-view identity, target-ray alignment, and yaw diversity lowers macro-F1 by 4.35 and 1.15 percentage points at eight and twelve actions. Removing the candidate-observability model lowers macro-F1 by 7.52 and 8.03 points and increases travel at both budgets. These findings connect the quality of the final decision to both the evidence retained in memory and the prediction of what another viewpoint can reveal.

The same evidence representation also helps under fixed observations. Across 36 HM3D scenes, every method receives identical RGB-D images and camera poses. SafeVantage achieves selective risk of 0.524, compared with 0.715 for caption-RAG, 0.617 for ConceptGraphs, and 0.618 for VLMaps, with lower risk also at matched answer coverage. In a controlled study of 73 ScanNet questions, restoring four target-visible views raises Qwen2-VL token F1 from 0.2483 to 0.4410. The question, reader, input ranks, and history length remain fixed, isolating the contribution of the supporting images.

A further evaluation keeps the method, thresholds, and evaluator fixed on 233 additional houses excluded from training, validation, and the original test. Macro-F1 improves by 7.3 and 4.3 points at eight and twelve actions, with lower risk and travel and higher answer rates at both horizons. This additional holdout supports the transfer of the acquisition strategy to new environments.

Together, the studies show how claim-level visual evidence can guide both observation gathering and semantic decisions. Supporting views provide positive evidence, while search coverage helps assess whether missing detections justify a negative answer. The current evaluation focuses on category-presence queries and discrete viewpoints; continuous navigation, real-robot sensing, and richer questions about attributes and relations are the next settings to investigate.
